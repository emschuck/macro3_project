\section{The CFA Franc Zone}
The CFA franc zone is one of the longest-running fixed exchange rate arrangements in the global economy. It links a group of West and Central African countries to an external anchor currency through two monetary unions: the West African Economic and Monetary Union (WAEMU) and the Central African Economic and Monetary Community (CEMAC). The institutional architecture has historically rested on four central features: a fixed parity against the anchor currency, convertibility guarantee by the French Treasury, free capital mobility, and the pooling of foreign exchange reserves.These features have made the CFA franc zone an important case study in international macroeconomics because it combines monetary credibility and exchange-rate stability with a substantial loss of nominal exchange rate flexibility and monetary policy autonomy. The central policy question is whether this arrangement has supported or constrained economic performance. 

\section{Replication of the Original Study}

In their article, Alassane Diallo and Adama Ba sought to explore the impact of the peg from the French franc to the euro on the economies of the countries in the CFA-zone. There are a lot of conflicts in the literature about whether they have experienced a stronger or weaker growth after the peg shift, compared to what would have been without France joining the euro-zone. On one hand we could expect a high growth rate due to the access to an intensive trade market with the European Economic Community (Dearden, 1999; Hadjimichael and Galy, 1997). But on the other hand there are many uncertainties linked to the consequence of being pegged to a strong currency like the euro, this could undermine expectations following the switch of anchor currencies (Zafar, 2005). 
At the time of the peg switch, in 2002, a report of the French Senate stated that there was no comprehensive study that would prove that the peg of the CFA-Franc to the euro would be an advantage or a drawback for member countries. 
Therefore, the authors of the paper wanted to address this gap by estimating the impact of the euro changeover on CFA-zone economies. Their goal was first to allow for more empirical evidence to strengthen the debates but also to inform CFA-zone policy makers. For their analysis they chose to use GDP per capita as the outcome variable of interest. 

Diallo and Ba (2024) use the synthetic control method (Abadie, 2021) to construct a proxy GDP per capita for each CFA-countries, meaning an estimate of what the growth would have been without the peg changeover. This is done through the construction of a weighted average of comparable non-CFA countries, based on pre-treatment characteristics. This method provides a transparent comparison group for our treated countries, and it can produce a more credible counterfactual than with a single control country. 

The thirteen treated countries of the CFA-zone are divided between two economical communities: the West African Economic and Monetary Community (WAEMU) and the Central African Economic and Monetary Community (CAEMC), as shown in Table \ref{tab:treated_countries}.

The donor countries all have fixed exchange rates regimes and have been matched based on other economic or institutional characteristics. The authors first included African countries with similar socioeconomic characteristics as the treated countries (Botswana, Eswatini, Lesotho, Mauritius, Morocco, Namibia and Seychelles) and they then added other countries with the similar level of development (Bangladesh, Barbados, Bhutan, Bolivia, Dominican Republic, Ecuador, Grenade, Laos, Oman, Panama, Saint Kitts \& Nevis and Saint-Lucia). We have chosen to add three countries that are also similar to allow for more precise results (Turkmenistan, Guyana and Iraq). 

The treatment date is 2002, as it was the date of the French Franc to Euro peg changeover. This means that the synthetic control weights will be computed on the similarities of the characteristics from 1980 to 2002. 

The paper finds positive post-treatment output gaps for several countries, however those results are not statistically significant for any countries, except for Equatorial Guinea. 

\section{Replication Methodology and Data}

The replication follows the original paper's country-level synthetic control design. For each treated CFA franc country, we estimate a counterfactual GDP path using a weighted average of non-CFA donor countries, and then compare the observed post-2002 GDP path with this synthetic counterfactual. The treatment year, time period, and country sample all follow the original paper's specifications, while variable selection and construction, and missing data approaches diverged. Further details on the variable construction, and missing-data treatment are reported in Appendix~\ref{app:replication_data}: Table~\ref{tab:variable_construction}, and Table~\ref{tab:missing_data_treatment}.

Let \(Y_{it}\) denote GDP per capita for country \(i\) in year \(t\). For a treated country \(i=1\), the unobserved counterfactual outcome after treatment is approximated using a weighted average of \(J\) donor countries:

\begin{equation}
\widehat{Y}_{1t}^{N}
=
\sum_{j=2}^{J+1} w_j Y_{jt},
\label{eq:synthetic_outcome}
\end{equation}

where the donor weights satisfy the standard synthetic control constraints:

\begin{equation}
w_j \geq 0,
\qquad
\sum_{j=2}^{J+1} w_j = 1.
\label{eq:weight_constraints}
\end{equation}

The weights are chosen to make the synthetic control resemble the treated country as closely as possible before treatment. Let \(X_1\) denote the vector of pre-treatment characteristics for the treated country, and let \(X_0\) denote the corresponding matrix for the donor countries. The donor-weight vector \(W\) is selected to minimise the distance between the treated country and the weighted donor average:

\begin{equation}
\min_W
\left(X_1 - X_0 W\right)' V \left(X_1 - X_0 W\right),
\label{eq:scm_minimization}
\end{equation}

subject to the constraints in Equation~\ref{eq:weight_constraints}. The matrix \(V\) determines the relative importance assigned to each predictor in matching the treated country to the donor pool. In the reconstructed specification, the predictors include agriculture, government share, investment share, official development assistance, foreign direct investment, labour, institutional quality, and selected pre-treatment GDP values. Industry is excluded from the final specification because of missingness and data-quality concerns, as discussed in Appendix~\ref{app:replication_issues}.

The estimated treatment effect is the post-treatment gap between the observed outcome and the synthetic counterfactual:

\begin{equation}
\widehat{\alpha}_{1t}
=
Y_{1t}
-
\widehat{Y}_{1t}^{N}
=
Y_{1t}
-
\sum_{j=2}^{J+1} w_j Y_{jt},
\qquad t \geq 2002.
\label{eq:treatment_effect}
\end{equation}

A positive value of \(\widehat{\alpha}_{1t}\) indicates that actual GDP per capita was above the synthetic counterfactual, while a negative value indicates that actual GDP per capita was below the counterfactual. The interpretation of these gaps depends on the quality of the pre-treatment fit: if the synthetic control does not reproduce the treated country's pre-2002 path closely, the post-treatment gap is less informative.

The replication is best understood as a reconstructed replication rather than a mechanical reproduction of the original paper. Two issues were especially important. First, the GDP series used in the original paper could not be identified exactly from the published documentation. Second, the paper does not report how missing observations were handled, even though missing outcomes or predictors can affect donor eligibility, predictor averages, and estimated synthetic controls. These issues are discussed in Appendix~\ref{app:replication_issues}; the final variable definitions are summarised in Table~\ref{tab:variable_construction}, and the missing-data procedures are summarised in Table~\ref{tab:missing_data_treatment}.

The analysis proceeds in four steps:

Several additional reporting issues in the original paper also shaped the replication strategy. These include donor-pool ambiguity, inconsistent country naming, unclear variable definitions, inflation-source inconsistencies, table-label errors, and numerical values that are difficult to reconcile with the stated data structure. These issues do not invalidate the paper's research question, but they reduce confidence that the published tables provide sufficient information for an exact replication. The purpose of the replication is therefore to evaluate the robustness of the paper's broad conclusions using a transparent, reproducible data construction and estimation procedure.

Both figures are tools to compare the growth that actually happened with the treatment and our proxy growth. In our result, we found that almost all countries showed increasingly large negative GDP per capita gaps as the actual output fell below our counterfactual, with only Equatorial Guinea having a positive output gap. This suggests that the changeover to a euro peg had negative effects on the countries of the CFA-zone. Our results can be explained by an overvaluation of their currency because of the peg to a strong euro. This would make trade more difficult for our treated countries. However we cannot draw those conclusions using only the GDP per capita as it has multiple different input, it would thus be interesting to overview another output that is more closely related to trade, for example imports and exports. 

However our results conflict with the one of the original paper. Indeed, Diallo \& Ba (2024) found a positive output gap for multiple countries, for instance Chad, the Central African Republic or Mali. Thus their result would suggest that the peg of CFA Franc to the euro may boost the real GDP per capita compared to the synthetic output. Due to the data and methodology issues in the original paper, we cannot be sure about the exact reasons for this discrepancy, but it is likely that the different GDP series, missing-data treatments, or errors in the original implementation played a major role.

\subsection{Placebo testing}
Figure~\ref{fig:scm_placebo_pvalues} presents the results of our placebo significance testing. We followed the in-place placebo method, which consists of analysing each donor country as if it had received the treatment. Then we have estimated the synthetic control for each donor country and we can then compute the output gap for each country. The aim of this approach is to compare the difference between the pre-treatment and post-treatment output gap of each treated country with the analogous output gaps of the donor countries. 

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{../output/figures/scm_placebo_pvalues_rmspe_ratio.png}
    \caption{Placebo p-values for the synthetic control method}
    \label{fig:scm_placebo_pvalues}
\end{figure}

The p-values are computed using the post/pre Root Mean Square Per Error ratios. The ratio is computed for all countries, and we then rank the results of the donor countries. Then we can observe how unusual, relatively, the ratio of each country is by seeing how low or high in the ranking it is, with the share of countries with a more extreme ratio determining the placebo p-values. 

With this method, we obtained the same results as the authors of the original paper. At the 5 and 10\% level, we find that only the result of Equatorial Guinea was statistically significant, while all the other treated countries do not have any significant results. 

\subsubsection{Equatorial Guinea}
Equatorial Guinea is an unusual case. In Figure~\ref{fig:equatorial_guinea_gap} we can observe the result for its synthetic control, with the output gap. And indeed we observe a consistently positive GDP per capita gap. We can also note that those results are the one that match the most those of the original paper, in terms of closeness of the numbers of the output gap.
In Figure~\ref{fig:equatorial_guinea_placebo_gaps}, we can observe graphically that those results are significant. 
This suggests that the changeover to the euro peg had a significantly positive impact on Equatorial Guinea. However it is important to highlight that Equatorial Guinea is a very singular country. Indeed the authors of the original paper reference studies that show an undervaluation of its exchange rates (Feindouno et al., 2020). We can also acknowledge that there was a large discovery of oil in Equatorial Guinea's land. Since then it has become one of Sub-Saharan Africa's largest oil producers. Because of the closeness of this event and the changeover to the euro peg, we cannot conclude on if the changeover had an impact on the growth of the country. The economic boom can be easily explained by their development on the oil market, thus bringing a significant bias in our analysis.

\appendix \section{Issues in the Original Paper and Implications for Replication} \label{app:replication_issues}
\subsection{Primary issues}

A major obstacle in the replication was identifying the GDP variable used in the original paper. Although the paper describes the outcomes as real GDP per capita, the reported values could not be matched to any standard GDP series available in either WDI or Penn World Table. We tested the full set of WDI GDP per capita measures, PWT output- and expenditure-side GDP variables, and constructed alternatives combining WDI aggregate GDP measures with PWT population to generate per-capita series. None of these reproduced the GDP values reported in the original tables. This created a fundamental reproducibility problem, because GDP per capita is both the main outcome and a key lagged predictor in the SC specification. We therefore treated the GDP series as an empirical reconstruction choice, rather than a directly observed replication input, selecting WDI GDP per capita in current US dollars, because it provided the closest correspondence to the published synthetic control patterns. 

This is important because synthetic control estimation requires a usable pre-treatment panel: missing values in the outcome or predictors can change which countries are eligible donors, alter predictor averages, and therefore affect the estimated synthetic counterfactual. In reconstructing the GDP analysis, missingness was substantial for several variables used or discussed in the original specification, especially sectoral predictors and some macro controls. The paper does not explain whether missing observations were dropped, interpolated, extrapolated, filled from alternative sources, or otherwise adjusted. We therefore had to make these choices explicitly. The final replication keeps both unimputed and imputed panels, fills selected gaps using documented rules, supplements agriculture with an additional source where possible, and drops industry from the final SCM specification because retaining it created serious data-availability and quality problems. 

These two primary issues have major implications for the methods and results of this analysis, and also have many possible approaches. As such, we did not expect to be able to exactly match the results of the paper, but rather aimed to produce a transparent reconstruction of the analysis, with clearly documented choices.

\subsection{Secondary Issues}
In addition to the missing methodology for missing data, as well as the unclear source reporting and unidentifiable GDP data, there are a number of smaller issues identified in the paper. Some of these issues have moderate implications for the results, while some should have no major impact. However, the number of directly observable mistakes in the paper substantially reduces confidence in the methods and underlying work that are not described or explicitly shown.

\textit{Inflation source}

The original paper describes the inflation data coming from WDI, however the published table values match the Penn World Table deflator-based series. This is unexpected, given the paper's text explicitly frames the inflation comparison as being calculated from WDI values. This issues raised questions about the sources of other variables, when replication with the listed sources was not possible. 

\textit{Donor-country naming: Dominica vs Dominican Republic}

The original paper's donor-pool description refers to the “Dominican Rep.”, while the synthetic control weights table lists “Dominica”. There are different countries, with different ISO codes, economic countries, and data histories. In the replication, we chose to align with the donor countries listed in the table and use Dominica.

\textit{Donor-pool size}

The paper states that each treated country is matched using 21 control countries, but the donor-weight table only lists 20 countries. This brings some uncertainty about the exact donor countries used in the published analysis.

\textit{DRC label in CFA-zone tables}

One of the published predictor-balance tables labels a treated country as “DRC”. The Democratic Republic of Congo is not a CFA franc-zone country and is not part of WAEMU or CEMAC. This is likely a mislabelling of the Republic of Congo, but it is also perhaps an indication that the wrong country was used as a treated country in the analysis. The replication uses explicit ISO3 country codes throughout to avoid ambiguity.

\textit{Devaluation dummy}

The paper reports a 1994 CFA devaluation dummy as part of the predictor balance, but the values do not appear consistent with a dummy variable averaged over the stated pre-treatment period. Additionally, Senegal is reported as zero despite being a CFA country in 1994. For this reason, the devaluation dummy is not retained as a final SCM predictor in the replication. 

\textit{Repeated GDP values in selected years}

The GDP values reported in Appendix 4 of the paper repeat exactly across different years for the same country. This is clearly a mistake in the table, but means that one of the two columns is missing. This error was made more challenging by the inability to find their reported numbers through any calculation method with their listed sources. 

\textit{Investment variable definition}

The paper's variable appendix appears to describe investment as a household-consumption share. This is inconsistent with the usual interpretation of investment of gross fixed capital formation. Since investment is an important growth predictor, this creates uncertainty over the exact variable used in the analysis: whether an incorrect variable was used, or whether the correct variables was used but not correctly reported. 

\textit{Sectoral variable sources}

The paper lists agriculture and industry as coming from WDI and Penn World Table, but Penn World Table does not provide the same standard sectoral value-added shares used in WDI. This makes the source documentation ambiguous. In the replication, agriculture is primarily taken from WDI and supplemented with FAOSTAT where needed, while industry is dropped because of missingness and data-quality problems.

\textit{OLS appendix observation counts}

The paper's OLS appendix reports 751 observations for country-specific regressions over 1990-2019. For 13 countries over 29 years, we would expect 377 observations. It is unclear what the 751 observations are, or how this table was generated, but this value suggest some error in calculation or duplication. 

\textit{FDI copy error in OLS table}

The paper's OLS appendix has the reported FDI coefficient for Senegal as -43.7, vastly out of scale with the reported FDI coefficients for every other country (all between 15816 and 17245). Additionally, this value and its standard error match exactly the value for the ODA coefficient, suggesting there are errors in the table generation, possibly mistakenly copying the wrong value in.

\textit{Post-treatment RMSPE wording}

The methodology section states that weights are chosen to minimise post-treatment RMSPE, but standard synthetic control methods chose weights using pre-treatment information only. Using post-treatment outcomes to choose weights would violate the logic of the method. This is likely a wording error, but would substantially change the method and results if it were the case. 

\textit{Inflation table period label}

The first column of the WAEMU inflation table appears to be incorrectly labelled. The table header indicates 1980-2021, while the other tables and surrounding text reference the pre-euro and post-euro period. These numbers do not match those identified by our replication for either the pre-euro, or total periods. Our ability to match the other inflation table values, except for the WAEMU section, paired with the incorrect label, suggests there may have been a mistake in the table creation or value calculations.

\textit{Zero inflation value for Sao Tome and Principe}

The non-CFA inflation table reports Sao Tome and Principe as having exactly zero average inflation over the pre-period. This reflects missing data treatment rather than an actual inflation average, and the replication exercise finds a positive value here (31.7\%) along with alignment with the rest of the table. This is one instance where missing value treatment is not explained but may have impacts on the results of the paper. 

\textit{Synthetic control weights reported as percentage points}

The paper describes the synthetic-control weights as being expressed in percentage points, but the values in the table are decimal weights. This is just a terminology issues, but matters for the interpretation of the table.
